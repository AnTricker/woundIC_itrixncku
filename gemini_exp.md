---

## Summary: Features of Abrasion Wounds

Abrasions, commonly known as scrapes or "road rash," occur when the skin is rubbed against a rough surface. Their primary features include:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Depth** | Superficial; they usually only affect the epidermis and sometimes the upper dermis. |
| **Bleeding** | Minimal; often characterized by "oozing" from capillaries rather than a steady flow. |
| **Pain** | High; because they expose many sensitive nerve endings in the skin. |
| **Appearance** | Often look like "pitting" or "streaking" (linear patterns) based on the direction of friction. |
| **Fluid** | Frequent "weeping" of clear or yellowish fluid (plasma) during the initial healing phase. |

---

## Summary: Features of Bruise Wounds (Contusions)

Unlike abrasions, bruises (contusions) are internal injuries where the skin remains unbroken. They are characterized by:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Integrity** | The epidermis remains **intact**; there is no open wound or external bleeding. |
| **Color Cycle** | Changes over time: Red (initial) → Blue/Purple (1-2 days) → Green/Yellow (5-10 days) → Brown. |
| **Cause** | Result from **blunt force trauma** that ruptures capillaries, causing blood to leak into the surrounding tissue. |
| **Swelling** | Often accompanied by localized swelling (edema) or a raised lump (hematoma). |
| **Tenderness** | Usually painful to the touch due to pressure on nerve endings from the trapped blood and inflammation. |

---

## Summary: Features of Burn Wounds

Burns are tissue damage resulting from heat, chemicals, electricity, or radiation. They are medically categorized by depth:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **First-Degree** | Superficial; limited to the epidermis. Causes redness, pain, and minor swelling (e.g., mild sunburn). |
| **Second-Degree** | Partial-thickness; affects epidermis and dermis. Characterized by **blisters**, intense pain, and a "weeping" or moist appearance. |
| **Third-Degree** | Full-thickness; extends through all skin layers. May look white, charred, or leathery. Often painless because nerve endings are destroyed. |
| **Inflammation** | Nearly all burns involve a "zone of hyperemia" (redness) where blood flow increases to the damaged area. |
| **Fluid Loss** | Significant burns lead to the formation of blisters as plasma leaks from damaged capillaries into the space between skin layers. |

---

## Summary: Features of Cut Wounds (Incisions & Lacerations)

Cuts are classified as either **incisions** (clean edges from sharp objects) or **lacerations** (jagged edges from blunt trauma). Their primary features include:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Edges** | Can be **smooth and regular** (incisions) or **jagged and torn** (lacerations). |
| **Bleeding** | Often **heavy and immediate** because sharp objects easily sever blood vessels. |
| **Depth** | Varies greatly; can involve the epidermis, dermis, and deeper structures like fat or muscle. |
| **Gaping** | Deep cuts often "gape" open, meaning the edges do not stay together without assistance (like stitches). |
| **Pain** | Typically causes a **sharp, stinging pain** due to the direct severing of nerve endings. |

---

## Summary: Features of Ingrown Nails & Paronychia

Ingrown nails occur when the edge of the nail grows into the surrounding skin, leading to an inflammatory condition called paronychia. Their primary features include:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Erythema** | Intense **redness** of the skin folds surrounding the nail. |
| **Edema** | **Swelling** that can make the skin appear shiny or tense. |
| **Purulence** | The formation of **pus** (abscess), which may be white, yellow, or green. |
| **Granulation** | In chronic cases, the body may grow extra "fleshy" red tissue (granuloma) over the nail edge. |
| **Hypertrophy** | The skin fold may thicken and grow over the nail plate as a defensive response. |

---

## Summary: Features of Lacerations

Lacerations are wounds produced by the tearing of soft body tissue. Unlike incisions, they are typically caused by blunt force trauma. Their primary features include:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Edges** | Characteristically **jagged, irregular, and torn**; they do not have the "clean" look of a knife cut. |
| **Cause** | Usually caused by **blunt force** (hitting an object) or machinery accidents. |
| **Tissue Bridging** | Often contains "bridges" of intact blood vessels or nerves across the wound bed (a key differentiator from incisions). |
| **Contamination** | Higher risk of infection because the blunt force often pushes dirt or debris into the irregular edges. |
| **Associated Injuries** | Frequently accompanied by **bruising** (contusions) or swelling in the surrounding area. |

---

## Summary: Features of Puncture/Stab Wounds

Puncture wounds, often referred to as stab wounds, are caused by a pointed object. Their primary features include:

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Depth vs. Width** | The wound is typically **deeper than it is wide** on the skin surface. |
| **Bleeding** | Often shows **minimal external bleeding**, which can be deceptive as internal bleeding may be significant. |
| **Infection Risk** | High; because the wound is deep and narrow, it is difficult to clean and can trap bacteria (like tetanus) inside. |
| **Entry Point** | Usually a small, circular, or slit-like opening that may close over quickly. |
| **Internal Damage** | Can cause significant damage to underlying structures (nerves, tendons, or organs) without large visible surface trauma. |
